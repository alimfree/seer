Every NEMT operator can tell you their denial rate or their average trip value. Almost none can tell you how many calls their office didn't answer last month, or what those calls were worth. That gap matters, because an unanswered call isn't a neutral non-event. It's usually a booked trip that happened somewhere else, with a competitor who picked up.

## Why a missed call is a lost trip, not just a lost call

A rider or facility scheduler calling to book a ride is rarely calling only one operator. If your line rings through to voicemail, especially outside business hours, the default behavior isn't "wait for a callback." It's "call the next number on the list." By the time your office reopens and returns the message, the trip is often already booked elsewhere, and the caller has no reason to switch back. The lost value isn't the call itself, it's the private-pay or facility-contract trip, and often the recurring series of trips that would have followed it.

## The three inputs that actually determine your number

Most operators either don't estimate this cost at all, or estimate it off a guess. The actual number comes from three inputs you can measure:

**After-hours and overflow call volume.** How many calls come in when your office is closed, or when staff are already on another line? Your phone system or CAD platform's call log usually has this, even if nobody has looked at it before.

**Your answer rate on those calls.** Of the calls that come in after-hours or during a rush, what share actually reach a person versus going to voicemail? Most operators are surprised by how low this is once they check, because staffing a phone line evenings and weekends is expensive and rarely fully covered.

**Average trip value and, critically, series length.** A single one-way ambulatory trip is worth less than a recurring dialysis or physical therapy series booked three times a week for months. Missing the first call in a series costs you the whole series, not one trip.

## A simple back-of-envelope formula

Multiply missed after-hours calls per month by your estimated booking rate on answered calls (the share of callers who actually book when someone picks up), then multiply by average trip value. That gives a rough monthly revenue-at-risk number. Most operators who run this for the first time land somewhere in the thousands of dollars a month, concentrated in evenings, weekends, and the first hour after your office opens when overnight voicemails finally get returned.

## Where the number is usually worse than operators expect

Three windows account for most of the loss: **after 5 PM on weekdays**, when staff go home but private-pay demand doesn't stop; **weekends**, when many smaller operators run with no phone coverage at all; and **peak dispatch hours**, when your existing staff are on other calls and new inbound callers simply ring out. That last one is easy to miss, because it doesn't look like an after-hours problem on paper, it looks like being busy, but the revenue impact is identical.

## Two leaks that compound the cost

**Callers who don't leave a voicemail.** Voicemail abandonment on cold inbound calls is high. A caller who doesn't reach a person and doesn't bother leaving a message never shows up in your callback list at all, which means your voicemail count understates your actual missed-call volume.

**Recurring series that quietly evaporate.** If a facility scheduler calls to book a new recurring series and can't get through, they don't just lose one trip's worth of business from you, they route the entire series to whichever operator did answer. This is why after-hours coverage gaps disproportionately hurt operators competing for facility contracts over one-off private-pay rides.

## What operators do about it

The realistic options are roughly: absorb the gap and accept the lost revenue, staff an on-call rotation (which solves coverage but adds payroll and burnout risk), use a generic answering service (which gets a human on the line but usually can't book directly into your dispatch system), or use an AI voice agent that answers every call and writes bookings straight into your CAD. Which makes sense depends on your call volume and how much of it is currently going unanswered, which is exactly what the calculator below estimates for your specific numbers.

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I find out how many calls I'm actually missing?**
Most phone systems and many CAD platforms log call volume and outcome (answered, voicemail, abandoned) even if no one has pulled the report before. Start there before estimating anything from memory, since actual missed-call volume is almost always higher than operators guess.

**Does this only matter for private-pay operators?**
No. Facility schedulers booking Medicaid broker trips also call around when one operator can't be reached, and a missed call there costs you a contract relationship, not just a single fare.

**Is a generic answering service enough to fix this?**
It gets a human voice on the line, which is better than voicemail, but most answering services take a message rather than actually booking the trip and confirming it back to the caller in real time. That still leaves a callback delay during which the caller may book elsewhere.

**How much after-hours call volume justifies paying for coverage?**
There's no universal threshold, but the math above answers it for you: once estimated monthly revenue-at-risk exceeds the cost of covering the gap, whether by staffing or by an automated answering solution, the coverage pays for itself.
