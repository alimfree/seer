Incident-to billing is the mechanism that allows primary care practices to use clinical staff — nurses, medical assistants, care coordinators — to deliver RPM services while billing under the supervising physician's NPI. It is how most practices make RPM financially viable, since having a physician personally spend 20+ minutes per patient per month reviewing device data is not a scalable use of their time.

But incident-to rules are specific, and violating them turns legitimate billing into a compliance liability. This guide covers what incident-to means in the context of RPM, which staff qualify, what supervision looks like in practice, how state rules vary, and what documentation you need to survive an audit.

## What Incident-To Means for RPM

In plain terms, incident-to means that services provided by auxiliary personnel can be billed under a physician's NPI as if the physician provided them — but only when specific conditions are met. For RPM, this applies to CPT codes 99457 and 99458, which involve clinical staff time reviewing physiologic data and communicating with patients.

### The Three Requirements for Incident-To Billing

Every incident-to claim must satisfy three conditions simultaneously:

| Requirement | What It Means for RPM |
|-------------|----------------------|
| 1. Physician initiated the plan of care | The supervising physician must have established the RPM order, including the monitoring parameters and clinical thresholds for intervention |
| 2. Physician provides direct supervision | The physician must be present in the office suite (not necessarily the same room) and immediately available during the time the staff member is performing RPM services |
| 3. Services are integral to the physician's professional service | The RPM activities performed by staff must be the type of services commonly furnished in a physician's office and integral to the physician's ongoing management of the patient |

All three must be true at the time the service is provided. If any one is missing, the claim cannot be billed incident-to.

> **Critical clarification:** "Direct supervision" for incident-to purposes means the physician must be present in the office suite and immediately available to provide assistance and direction. It does not mean the physician must be watching over the staff member's shoulder. The physician can be seeing their own patients in another exam room — as long as they are physically present in the same office suite and available if needed.

## Which Staff Can Perform RPM Services Incident-To

Not every employee qualifies as "auxiliary personnel" for incident-to purposes. CMS defines auxiliary personnel as anyone who works under the supervision of a physician, regardless of whether they are employees of the physician or of the practice.

### Eligible Staff for RPM Incident-To Billing

| Staff Type | Can Bill Incident-To? | Notes |
|-----------|----------------------|-------|
| Registered Nurse (RN) | Yes | Most common RPM staff type; can also bill independently under own NPI in some states |
| Licensed Practical Nurse (LPN/LVN) | Yes | Qualified for incident-to; cannot bill independently |
| Medical Assistant (MA) | Yes | Must be working under direct physician supervision; scope varies by state |
| Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA) | Yes | Less common for RPM; typically used in long-term care settings |
| Care Coordinator (clinical background) | Yes | Must hold a clinical credential (RN, LPN, MA); title alone is not sufficient |
| Care Coordinator (non-clinical) | No | A care coordinator without clinical credentials cannot perform billable RPM services |
| Front Desk / Administrative Staff | No | Non-clinical staff cannot perform RPM clinical services |
| Physician Assistant (PA) | Situational | PAs can bill under their own NPI at 85% of the physician rate; incident-to billing allows 100% but requires direct supervision |
| Nurse Practitioner (NP) | Situational | Same as PA — can bill independently or incident-to depending on preference and state rules |

### The PA and NP Decision

PAs and NPs can bill under their own NPI at 85% of the physician rate or incident-to the supervising physician at 100%. The math usually favors incident-to when the physician is routinely present:

| Billing Method | 99457 Reimbursement | 99458 Reimbursement | Monthly Difference (per patient) |
|---------------|--------------------|--------------------|----------------------------------|
| Under PA/NP NPI (85%) | ~$44.35 | ~$35.89 | Baseline |
| Incident-to physician NPI (100%) | ~$52.18 | ~$42.22 | +$14.16 |

For 100 RPM patients, incident-to billing for PAs and NPs generates approximately $1,400 more per month compared to billing under their own NPI. Over a year, that is nearly $17,000 in additional revenue.

## Direct Supervision: What It Actually Requires

Direct supervision is the most frequently misunderstood — and most frequently violated — component of incident-to billing. Here is what it does and does not require.

### What Direct Supervision Requires

- The supervising physician must be **physically present in the office suite** during the time RPM services are being performed.
- The physician must be **immediately available** to provide assistance and direction if needed. "Immediately available" means the physician can step away from what they are doing and provide guidance within minutes — not within hours.
- The physician must be a **physician** — MD or DO. CMS does not allow NPs or PAs to serve as the supervising provider for incident-to purposes (though some state laws and commercial payer rules differ).

The physician does not need to be in the same room, review every data point, co-sign every note, or be involved in every patient communication.

### Remote Staff: The Common Pitfall

If your RPM clinical staff works from home, you cannot bill those services incident-to the physician. A nurse reviewing RPM data from home while the physician is at the clinic does not meet the direct supervision requirement. Options: bill under the staff member's own NPI (if they have one), require on-site RPM work, or split workflows so on-site staff handles billable activities.

> **Audit red flag:** If your billing records show RPM services billed incident-to the physician on days when the physician was not in the office (vacation, conference, hospital rounding days), this is a serious compliance problem. Payers and OIG auditors can cross-reference physician schedules with claim dates.

## State Variations in Incident-To Rules

While Medicare's incident-to rules are federal, state scope-of-practice laws affect which staff can perform RPM services and under what conditions. Here are the most significant state-level variations.

### Medical Assistant Scope of Practice

| State Category | MA Scope for RPM | Examples |
|---------------|-----------------|----------|
| Broad scope | MAs can review RPM data, communicate with patients about readings, and document findings under physician supervision | Texas, Florida, Arizona |
| Moderate scope | MAs can collect and document RPM data but patient communication must be performed by an RN or higher | California, New York, Illinois |
| Restrictive scope | MAs are limited to administrative tasks; RPM clinical activities require licensed nursing staff | Some New England states |

The distinction matters because if your state restricts MA scope of practice, having MAs perform clinical RPM activities — even under direct physician supervision — could violate state law regardless of what Medicare's incident-to rules permit.

Additionally, some states define "direct supervision" differently from CMS — requiring the physician to be on the same floor, requiring filed supervision agreements, or setting staff-to-physician ratios. In states with full NP practice authority, incident-to billing for NPs is optional. Always verify your state's supervision requirements in addition to CMS rules. The more restrictive standard applies.

## Documentation Requirements for Incident-To RPM

If your RPM services are audited, the auditor will look for documentation proving that incident-to requirements were met. Here is what you need to have in the record.

### Per-Service Documentation

For each RPM service billed incident-to, the medical record should contain:

| Documentation Element | Example |
|----------------------|---------|
| Name and credentials of the performing staff member | "Jane Smith, RN" |
| Date and time of the RPM activity | "March 15, 2026, 10:15 AM – 10:38 AM" |
| Description of the activity performed | "Reviewed patient's blood glucose data for March 1-15. Three readings above 250 mg/dL noted on March 8, 11, and 14. Called patient to discuss dietary factors and medication adherence." |
| Supervising physician name and NPI | "Supervised by Dr. Ahmed Khan, NPI 1234567890" |
| Confirmation of physician presence | "Supervising physician present in office suite during service" |

At the practice level, also maintain: signed RPM orders for each patient, a written supervision policy, current staff credential files, and a physician schedule log confirming presence on dates when incident-to services were billed.

## Common Incident-To Violations in RPM

**Billing when the physician is off-site.** The physician is at the hospital, at a conference, or working from home, but RPM claims are still billed under their NPI. Build a daily check into your billing workflow — verify the supervising physician was in the office before submitting claims for that date.

**No physician order for RPM.** Incident-to services must be performed under a physician's plan of care. No written RPM order means the incident-to billing chain is broken. Make the RPM order a mandatory step in your enrollment workflow.

**Using non-clinical staff for clinical RPM activities.** A staff member titled "care coordinator" but without clinical credentials cannot perform RPM clinical services. Verify credentials for every staff member assigned to RPM and maintain credential files.

**Missing time documentation.** Incident-to claims that lack specific time entries — start time, end time, total minutes — are indefensible in an audit. Use a time-tracking tool that records start and stop times rather than manual estimation.

## Building an Audit-Proof Workflow

Keep incident-to billing compliant by verifying physician presence daily before staff performs RPM work, matching staff to authorized activities based on credentials, tracking time with automated tools (not manual estimates), documenting the supervising physician's name in every RPM note, and auditing a sample of claims quarterly against physician schedules and staff credentials.
